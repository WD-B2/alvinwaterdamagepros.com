Commercial water losses differ from home losses in ways that affect how the job is run. Floors are larger, there are usually tenants or neighbors sharing walls, and the building is a working space with inventory, equipment and records. The goal is to get the building dry and usable again with as little disruption as possible.

Common Commercial Water Losses

Supply line and fixture failures

A burst line in a restroom or break room can flood a floor and the space below.

Roof and storm leaks

Flat or low-slope commercial roofs leak at seams, drains and rooftop units. Rain can run into ceilings, lights and stock.

Fire sprinkler discharge

A sprinkler head that opens by accident can release a lot of water over a big area.

Sewer and floor drain backups

Backups in restaurants and shops come with contamination and health code concerns.

What to Do If Water Is Spreading Through the Building

  1. Shut off the water at the main or the riser. If the source is a sprinkler, call your fire protection company.
  2. Turn off power to wet areas. Contact the electric utility if the main panel is affected.
  3. Move inventory, files and equipment off the floor onto pallets or shelves.
  4. Call your property manager or landlord if you lease. They may carry the building policy.
  5. Photograph the water, the source and damaged stock before moving it.
  6. Call the emergency line for extraction and drying.

Tenants, Landlords and Who Pays

In a leased building, responsibility for the building and for contents is usually split. The landlord's policy covers the structure. The tenant's policy covers contents, improvements and lost income. A water loss that crosses tenant lines can involve several policies and several adjusters. Read your lease and ask your insurer who handles what.

Keep records of everything you spend, including emergency supplies and overtime. If you lose business because you cannot open, keep records of the days closed and typical sales.

Documentation for a Commercial Claim

  • Dated photos of the source, the water and each affected room.
  • A list of damaged inventory with quantities and costs.
  • Daily drying logs and moisture readings.
  • Invoices for emergency work.
  • Copies of the lease and the policy.
  • A log of calls with your insurer and landlord.

Commercial adjusters often look for a clear scope that separates mitigation from repair. We keep both in writing and explain each line before work starts.

The industry reference behind commercial water damage work is the IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration, which we use as the yardstick when explaining drying goals and equipment.

Do I call my landlord or insurer first?

Both, but do not wait to stop the damage. Leases and policies assign responsibility differently. Take photos and keep receipts.

Can you work after hours?

Often yes. Tell the dispatcher your hours and the dispatcher will arrange the work to reduce disruption.

Does business insurance cover lost income?

Some policies include business interruption coverage. Ask your agent what applies.

Do you restore stock and documents?

Water-damaged papers and some contents can be dried. Some items may need to be discarded. Ask for a list before disposal.
